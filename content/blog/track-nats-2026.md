**DAY 1: THE TRAVEL**

This time it’s not so new. The MIT Cycling track team (Matteo Fulghieri) is traveling once more to Indiana to compete in the USAC Collegiate Track Nationals at the Major Taylor Velodrome. The flight into Indianapolis International Airport is every heavy cyclist’s daydream: a landscape flatter than my cast iron pan at home where I stir fry vegetables most Tuesday evenings.

Arriving in Indianapolis, the first step is to make my way to the eventual race site to sign in and register. Akin to a captain at his helm, with the stern facing the land he loves, I stand now with only my bike case and firmly planted feet facing a storm of uncertainty; I can’t know what the following three days will hold for me, but I do know they will occur, and their outcome forever a moment in time.

There’s even a sense of peculiarity; the precisely measured 333m track sandwiched by a highway, a BMX track, and a university. An unusual gathering place for young adults who enjoy riding bikes to gather and pedal harder than they’ve ever pedaled before.

Following this moment of heavy anticipation and retrospection, it’s time to swing by the hotel. It is fortunately surrounded by a multitude of gas stations, which people oft forget are prime fueling stations for cyclists as well. Nothing beats the energy of a $1.99 roll of crunchy donuts.

**DAY 2: THE SPRINTS**

Sprints day is the first day. Today is all about speed and acceleration, with little need to endure powers long enough to hurt. The seating for the match sprints is quite straight forward: how quickly can you cover 200m with a wind up.

The best part of TrackNats is making friends, and fortunately the Fort Lewis Cycling team adopted me into their family. So much so that the MIT name was formally added to their tent, and we even shared our equipment. After all, mi chainring es su chainring.

![](/img/blog/posts/track-nats-2026/image1.jpg)
_Photo courtesy of Karen Brower Photography_

The flying 200m starts with a strong push in the back to get you some speed up the gradual incline of the back straight. With this, you roll ever so slowly through the Start/Finish with 2 to go. The name of the game is building up your potential energy but preserving your legs to the fullest. It’s a bit of a shimmy to get your knees brushing the top railing; pushing and rolling at just the right times to give a momentum kick. Now, rolling towards the Start/Finish approaching your 1 to go lap, the power picks up. A smooth roll evolves into a little pressure on the down stroke, then pressing down like you mean it, then coming out of the saddle to pull on the bars a bit, then the back stroke gets a tug, until you’re at the top of the railing and pushing and tugging with every ounce of strength in the legs. Finally, you round Turn 2 and pass through the 200m mark and it’s time to sit back into the saddle and spin the lower extremities right off the torso and hold on for dear life, pushing 40mph through Turns 3 and 4. This year I crossed the finish line in 11.531s, putting me in 15th and to race the 2nd place qualifier.

The match sprints themselves remind me a bit of my wrestling days, if you take out the wrestling and replace it with cycling—okay maybe it’s not really similar at all. The two lap race to the finish always starts slow, with a lot of eye contact and sometimes some smiles. Coming through the last lap, it then picks up into a battle for position—I was bested by my opponent, who very carefully stole the inside line and kept me stuck at the barriers; besides the fact that he was incredibly quick on the pedals.

**DAY 3: THE IP AND POINTS RACE**

The 4km Individual Pursuit is not so dynamic. You start pedaling, you keep pedaling, and then around lap 5 of 12 you realize you went out too hard and your legs are screaming. I call this the first stage of the IP: **regret**. Lap 7 brings the next stage: **negotiation**. You tell yourself you can handle this and just need to keep going, that this really isn’t the worse thing ever and the finish line is approaching. Lap 9 brings **hopelessness**; you were wrong, you can’t keep doing this, your legs have reached unregistered pain levels. You can’t even see anymore and the baking of the track is the only item keeping you from crashing. Then, the final laps bring **existential contextualization**: what does it mean to be a cyclist, what does it mean to be in pain, can I be certain that my pain is real, do I have the free will to stop pedaling, is humanity doomed to eternal suffering, if I am not for myself who will pedal this bike? Finally the finish line approaches, and it seems quite fit to immediately collapse in the grass. Today was a PR in 5:01.

* This is an important moment to highlight a lesson I learned this day: having been adopted into the FLC family also meant total treatment as such, and that meant being screamed at each lap. In those moments of desperation, these forced me to pedal harder. Shout out to the team parents Mari and Drew.

![](/img/blog/posts/track-nats-2026/image2.jpg)

Of course, the rest of my time was spent recovering and yelling words of encouragement to my FLC teammates, who all excelled in their races.

The evening wrapped up with the points race. 36km in 35 minutes does not leave the brain with much time to do math, and math I did not do. I managed to secure a few points on the opening sprint, but failed to encourage the mind to join the breakaway, and this left me in 12th place.

**DAY 4: THE KILO AND SCRATCH**

A deep rain enveloped the sprawled Indiana landscape; a reflection of how my legs felt at this point. On the bright side, this meant sleeping in and hanging out around the house and working. Reality emerged around noon: the remaining races would all take place in the evening. The opening kilo saw me pulling my cleat out of the pedal once more, so details of the frantic effort to clip back in and pull the cranks off the bike will be spared.

Finally was the scratch race. This time just 12km at a blistering pace exceeding 50kph. At these speeds, reaction time becomes critical and knowing the moves to jump into is vital. The race ended together, with myself in 14th. The women’s points race followed; from the vantage point of the pit you can see the entire race, which makes for an exhilarating spectator experience. This race unfortunately saw the only crash of the weekend, which fortunately saw only scrapes and bruises as a result.

After all was said and done, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology cycling team earned the highest club team omnium points, earning the title of a National Championship program. The horizon is bright for the program, and the goals are only higher for next year. And of course the reunion of the FLC-MIT familia.
